About

AI Content Engineer. Technical Writer. Docs-as-Code Practitioner.

Background

I'm a technical writer who engineers systems, not just documents them. Seven years of experience building documentation for enterprise software, API platforms, and hardware products, and since December 2025, building the AI systems that generate content itself.

My superpower is translating complexity into clarity. Whether I'm writing an API guide for thousands of developers, building a Gemini Gem that automates style guide enforcement, or architecting a prompt engineering pipeline that generates narrative content for 122 unique characters, the core skill is the same: structured thinking, clear communication, and systems that scale.

I work in Markdown, Git, and TypeScript daily. I use AI coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Cline, Hermes Agent, Kilo) as force multipliers for both writing and engineering tasks. I believe docs-as-code is the only sustainable approach to technical content at scale, and I believe prompt engineering is the new technical writing.

I hold a BA in Screenwriting, Cum Laude, from Chapman University. My background in narrative structure directly informs my approach to AI content engineering, building systems that produce coherent, character-consistent, emotionally resonant content at scale.

Common Questions

I was a technical writer for seven years. In December 2025, I started building with AI coding agents. Six months later, I had redesigned an enterprise help site, built a PDF pipeline, created interactive product simulations, and shipped a narrative RPG engine with 122 characters and a custom LLM quest pipeline. I didn't switch careers. I applied the same skills to a new medium.

I know the resume raises questions. Here are the answers.

You've been a technical writer for 7 years. What qualifies you for an AI role?

The skills that made me good at docs-as-code are the same skills that make me good at prompt engineering: structured thinking, information architecture, and systems that scale. At Redwood, I built FlareGuard, a Gemini Gem suite that automated content validation, during Innovation Week. After that, I used Claude Code and Cursor daily to audit source code, build interactive demos, and restructure 200 articles. By the time I was laid off, AI wasn't a side project. It was my workflow.

You have a screenwriting degree, not a computer science degree.

Screenwriting teaches you to write for an audience that makes first impressions in seconds. You learn to read preferences, use visual cues, think holistically about narrative. You learn that people don't analyze structure, they feel it. That mindset transfers directly to building software. Any good code should account for the same things: who's reading this, what do they notice first, what makes them stay. I work audience-first, which isn't the traditional engineering mindset. But it's the right one for content systems, documentation, and anything where the user's experience is the product.

Can you actually code, or do you just use AI tools?

I use AI coding agents as technical partners. That's different from vibe coding. My workflow has a quality gate: the Ponytail framework, a lazy-senior-dev methodology, ensures code ships clean on first audit. I don't just describe what I want and hope it works. I read the code, trace the logic, understand the architecture, and direct the implementation. FlareGuard went from idea to production in a week. CoSH is 122 handcrafted NPCs with a custom LLM quest pipeline. These aren't AI demos. They're engineered systems.

What makes you different from other tech writers who say they do AI?

Most tech writers who claim AI experience are using ChatGPT to draft articles. I build the systems that generate content. CoSH has a five-layer pipeline: data models, prompt construction, LLM generation, output validation, and UI rendering. Each layer has a single responsibility. The validation layer prevents hallucination through key allowlists and entity cross-referencing. That's not prompt engineering. That's systems engineering applied to content generation.

Aren't you just prompt engineering?

Prompt engineering is one layer of what I do. The harder parts are designing the system around the prompt: what data goes in, how output is validated, how state propagates, how hallucination is caught and corrected programmatically. Anyone can write a prompt. Designing a pipeline where the prompt is one component among five, and the system works reliably at scale, that's the engineering.

Why you over someone with years of software engineering and AI experience?

That's the wrong comparison. A software engineer builds systems. A technical writer builds content. I build systems that generate content. That's a different job. Most SWEs don't understand information architecture, content strategy, or how users actually consume documentation. Most tech writers can't trace source code or design validation pipelines. I do both. At Redwood, I became the person PMs came to because I understood the product end to end, not because I could out-code the engineers. CoSH isn't a coding project with a story bolted on. It's a narrative system where every technical decision serves the story. That intersection is rare, and it's exactly what AI content engineering requires.
