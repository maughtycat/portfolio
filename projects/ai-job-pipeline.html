From manual, repetitive job applications to an automated pipeline that scores, tailors, and tracks, built and used during my own job search.

I was laid off in June 2026 and immediately started applying to jobs. I was applying manually, one at a time, tailoring each resume and cover letter by hand. It took hours per application. I was burning out and the market was brutal, 300+ applicants per posting was the going rate. So I built a tool to automate the parts that don't need a human. Over 4.5 months, the pipeline processed 414 tailored applications, about 21 per week, and helped me land 10 hiring-manager-round interviews and one accepted offer. This is the story of building it, using it, and what it taught me about AI workflow engineering.

The Situation

June 2026. Laid off, mortgage, unemployment benefits with a deadline. The market was brutal: open roles drew 300+ applications on average (Ashby data, reported by HR Dive). I was applying manually: read the posting, decide if it's a fit, figure out which resume bullets apply, draft a cover letter, track the application. Every single one took hours. I was burning out before I'd even gotten to the interview stage.

The pattern was obvious. The research and drafting steps were the same every time: parse the posting, evaluate fit, generate materials. The judgment calls (should I apply? does this actually match my skills?) were the human part. The rest was mechanical. So I built a tool to automate the mechanical parts.

The pipeline has three stages: parse a job posting into structured data, score how well it matches my profile, and generate tailored resume bullets and cover letters. A tracker logs every application with status and fit score. The tool handles the research and drafting. I handle the judgment and submission. That's not a limitation, it's the point.

What Changed

Job Parsing

The pipeline extracts structured data from job postings using an LLM. Feed it a URL or paste the text, and it pulls out company, role, location, salary range, required skills, preferred skills, years of experience, and responsibilities. Every job posting is formatted differently. Some are clean HTML, some are PDFs, some are walls of text in an ATS. The LLM handles the chaos. Output is validated JSON, not free-form text.

Relevance Scoring

Not keyword matching. The scorer evaluates fit holistically against my profile: skills, experience, preferences, and dealbreakers. It returns a score (0-100), a breakdown by category, a reasoning string, and red flags. A score of 70+ means worth applying. Below 50 means skip. The red flags surface dealbreakers before I waste time: "requires 8+ years SWE experience," "requires CS degree," "onsite only." I applied to 414 jobs. The scorer saved me from applying to hundreds more that weren't worth the effort.

Material Generation

Each application gets tailored resume bullets, a cover letter draft, and answers to every application-specific question the posting asks. "Why this company?", "Tell us about yourself", "Describe a time you failed", "What's your greatest strength?" — the generator reads the posting, identifies every question field, and produces answers that reference my actual experience. The cover letter learns my voice from a reference text. The resume bullets match the posting's language. I review and edit every output before submitting. The tool drafts. I judge.

Application Tracking

SQLite-backed tracker logs every application: company, role, date applied, status, fit score, salary range, notes. Stats command shows applications per week, response rates, and average fit scores. The tracker also exports RESEA-compliant CSV for unemployment audits. I used this in June 2026 to satisfy a RESEA audit without scrambling to reconstruct my search history.

Human-in-the-Loop Design

The pipeline is not fully automated. I send the job links, verify the generated materials, and manually apply through each portal. Application forms have anti-bot measures ("write the word octopi"), inconsistent fields, and subtle requirements that an automated agent would miss. The tool handles the research and drafting. I handle the judgment and submission. This is the difference between "I automated my job search" and "I built a workflow that automates the parts that don't need a human while keeping me in the loop for the parts that do."

Use Cases

Job Search at Scale

The primary use case: evaluating job postings, scoring relevance, and generating tailored materials. The pipeline processes a job posting in under 30 seconds. The tracker logs every application with status, fit score, and notes. Stats command shows applications per week, response rates, and average fit scores. Used daily for 4.5 months across 414 applications.

Unemployment Compliance (RESEA)

State unemployment agencies require documented proof of job search activity. RESEA audits demand company name, position, date applied, and method for every application. The tracker stores this data automatically and exports it as a RESEA-compliant CSV. I used this in June 2026 to satisfy an audit without scrambling to reconstruct my search history from memory.

Salary Negotiation Context

The tracker stores salary ranges for every posting. When negotiating an offer, I can quickly pull the salary data for similar roles I applied to: "Based on the 291 salary ranges I captured, the average listed range was $119K to $165K." Data-backed negotiation beats gut feeling.

Engineering Decisions

Why These Technologies

Python — strongest skill, AI-augmented workflow runs in Python, most LLM SDKs are Python-first. Not choosing Java or Go because they're "real engineering" languages. Choosing the tool that gets the job done fastest.

OpenRouter — user brings their own API key. No surprise billing for the repo owner. Supports dozens of models. If someone wants to use GPT-4, Claude, or Llama, they just change the model name.

SQLite — zero config, portable, sufficient for a single-user pipeline. No Postgres server to run, no Docker compose file to maintain. The database is one file you can email someone.

Pydantic — validates LLM structured output against schemas. If the LLM returns garbage, Pydantic catches it before it reaches the rest of the pipeline. This is the difference between "trust the LLM" and "verify the LLM."

Streamlit — fastest path from "works in CLI" to "looks good in browser." Not the most scalable framework, but this isn't a SaaS product. It's a portfolio demo.

argparse — stdlib. No click, no typer, no dependency needed for a CLI that does four things.

Why These Design Choices

Human-in-the-loop. The tool handles research and drafting. The human handles judgment and submission. Application forms have anti-bot measures and inconsistent fields that break automation.

Code Review

After the initial build, I ran an automated audit (ponytail-audit) that scans for over-engineering, dead code, and unnecessary abstractions. It flagged 4 findings across the codebase. I applied 3: removed an unused import, deleted a dead function that loaded a YAML file nobody read, and simplified a nested retry structure that was doing the same thing twice. I skipped 1: client caching, because creating a new OpenAI client 3 times per CLI run is irrelevant overhead. The audit took 5 minutes. The fixes took 2. The point isn't that the code was bloated — it's that a 10-line review caught things I built in a hurry, and I knew which ones to fix and which ones didn't matter. That judgment is the skill.

When the search concluded, I ran a second, deeper pass: 11 findings, 10 applied, one skipped. The keepers were data-integrity fixes: validating status input so one typo can't poison the tracker, failing loudly instead of printing fake success, and scoping updates so two applications to the same company don't overwrite each other. I also wired Pydantic validation in for real, so the tech table above describes code, not copywriting. Skipped: a dependency version floor I couldn't verify without installing old releases. Same lesson, bigger diff: knowing which findings matter is the skill.

Impact

The real numbers from 4.5 months of actual usage.

Pipeline Stats

414 tailored applications, June 1 – October 14, 2026

~21 applications per week (19.3-week search, 17 logged pause days)

15 first conversations (screens and first-round interviews)

10 advanced to hiring-manager rounds (67% conversation-to-HM)

2 processes went past the HM round (Harvey take-home, WWT panel)

1 offer accepted (World Wide Technology, mid-October 2026)

The Numbers That Matter

15 first conversations, and 10 of those reached the hiring manager (67%). Customer.io's screen ended after five minutes: the hiring manager required coding without AI tools, a filter that was not in the job description.

The pipeline didn't just increase volume. It increased the quality of each application. Every posting got a tailored resume, a tailored cover letter, and a relevance score before I decided whether to apply.

What It Didn't Fix

204 rejections, 190 of them before a human ever spoke to me. Companies that never looked at the materials. Roles that were stretches. Market conditions that made remote-only positions brutally competitive. The tool didn't magic away a tough market. It let me play the volume game without sacrificing quality.

Full Scope

I built an AI-powered job application pipeline in Python: job posting parser (LLM-powered structured extraction), relevance scorer (holistic fit evaluation with reasoning and red flags), material generator (tailored resume bullets, cover letters, and answers to every application-specific question), and SQLite-backed application tracker with RESEA audit export. The pipeline uses OpenRouter for LLM access (user brings their own key), Pydantic for output validation, and Streamlit for a visual dashboard. Human-in-the-loop design: the tool handles research and drafting, the human handles judgment and submission. 414 tailored applications over 4.5 months, ~21 per week, 10 hiring-manager rounds, 67% conversation-to-HM conversion. Code reviewed with automated audit. ~1,333 lines across 12 files. Tools: Python, OpenRouter, SQLite, Pydantic, Streamlit, Git.
