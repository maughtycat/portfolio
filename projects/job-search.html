The Situation

June 2026. Laid off, mortgage, unemployment benefits with a deadline. The second layoff in three years, which adds a specific kind of pressure: you have already survived this once, so there is no room for pretending it is not happening. I started applying the same week, the same way everyone does, by hand. Read the posting, decide on fit, pick the bullets, draft the letter, fill the form, log it. Hours per application, and the market was not rewarding the effort.

The difference this time was what was already on my machine. The AI habit started nearly two years before the layoff, and the agentic work six months before it. The next section makes that case with dates and exhibits, because the timeline is the whole point.

Why AI, Not the Hype

I started using AI in earnest in August 2024, the month Redwood hired me, ChatGPT and Gemini in the daily work. I started building with it personally in July 2025: AI-assisted songs with Veo-generated music video clips that I edited myself, roughly ten-song concept albums with plots, where I wrote the lyrics, the story, and the progression and AI handled structure and rhymes. The one I like most is a bilingual album about the history of Argentina told through one man and his family, written in Spanish I cannot speak. I took French in high school. I brought the research, the plot, and the love affair with Buenos Aires, and Copilot helped the lines sound native. I started using agentic AI in December 2025: Codex first, then Claude Code, then Cursor, which Redwood provided, all before the layoff, and Hermes Agent after it. Four milestones, every one of them before the June 2026 layoff, the first three before AI became the headline explanation for every tech cut that year. The sequence matters to me because it is the opposite of a bandwagon.

The exhibits are public. FlareGuard shipped into production at Redwood, in the documentation and education department, about 10 people, and the escalations it was built to catch dropped noticeably. CoSH, a solo-built React and TypeScript agent runtime, started in December 2025 and I am still building it in October 2026, a practice rather than a prop (see the CoSH case study). My GitHub stays audit-clean. Best-practices research I did turned into versioned workflow files I actually run. For tooling I chose OpenRouter over single-vendor options for token optimization, the same bring-your-own-key reasoning I wrote up in the pipeline case study.

The part I want highlighted is how I use AI, not just that I do. Guardrails: FlareGuard's quality scoring and hallucination detection. Validation schemas: CoSH's structured JSON validation, Pydantic in the pipeline. Good prompts and memory: versioned skills and workflow files. That is my engineering answer to vibe coding, and I only need to say it once. The market is telling employers the same thing: LinkedIn's 2026 Skills on the Rise list puts AI governance among its fastest-growing skills. That demand is about the practice, not a job title, and I am not claiming a title I do not have.

I am not arguing I was early. Organizational AI adoption was already at 88% by 2026. I am arguing the work started before the layoff and did not stop after it, and every claim above points at a page I already published.

Last Time vs This Time

In September 2023 I was laid off and started applying to Technical Writer roles by hand, one application at a time. It took 12 months, and I landed in August 2024. In June 2026 I was laid off again, same mortgage, and this time the search took 4.5 months to an accepted offer. Both searches were hard, in different ways, and the comparison only means something if I am honest about both markets.

I began applying in September 2023, months into what would become the record year for tech layoffs: 265,660 employees cut across 1,194 companies, tracked by layoffs.fyi. January 2023 alone lost more than 108,000 tech jobs. 2024's drop to 152,922 read as recovery on the chart, but US tech postings had already entered what Indeed Hiring Lab would later name a three-year hiring freeze, down 36% below their February 2020 level. The national median unemployment claim ran 8.9 weeks in 2023, per the BLS. Mine ran 52. And the Bureau of Labor Statistics had already projected 1% growth for technical writers.

This time the weather was different and in some ways worse. Layoffs.fyi tracked 133,217 tech employees cut from January to October 2026, already past all of 2025's 122,606. Challenger counted US technology cuts up 66% year over year through May 2026. Open roles now receive more than 300 applications on average, and applications per hire have tripled since 2021. The average tech job search in 2026 runs 9.7 months, per a United Way NCA survey of 1,000 US job seekers. I did not need a survey to believe it, but it is good to know I was not imagining the room.

What actually changed was on my side of the table. I sent 34 applications at technical-writer titles. One produced a recruiter screen, at Firecrawl. None produced an interview, and none produced an offer. The content and knowledge roles I applied to instead produced 63 applications and 3 conversations. The technical writing lane was not a funnel problem. It was closed.

I have wondered whether recruiters saw more AI than technical writer on my profile. That wondering has a fair basis: my resume and portfolio foreground the AI work. I do not know what was in anyone's head, and I am not going to pretend I do. What I know is the market explanation above, and that the offer came from an AI Native Engineering role where the recruiter reached out after reviewing my resume.

I am not writing off the craft. Seven years of documentation work is why I know what good content engineering looks like. The point is what the market for that title did, not what the work is worth.

The Approach

The target was 5 applications per weekday, which sounds small until you demand that every one of them is tailored. Score before applying: the pipeline reads the posting, scores fit against my profile with reasoning and red flags, and if the score says skip, the hour I would have spent goes somewhere real instead. The tool drafts the resume bullets, the cover letter, and the answers to the posting's questions. I judge every word before it leaves my hands. For the mechanics of how it is built, the full pipeline case study covers the architecture.

One timeline note so nothing is oversold: the workflow scripts ran throughout the search, from June onward. The public GitHub repo is the cleaned-up version I published on August 15, 2026.

Where It Got Real

Funnel numbers are abstractions. The conversations were not. Figma's hiring manager screen, and what the follow-up email revealed about how early-stage teams actually staff knowledge work. Harvey's take-home and deep dive, the hardest I worked for any company in this search. HopSkipDrive, whose 10-slide presentation happened inside the hiring manager round rather than after it. MRO's three calls across three weeks. Mercury's hiring manager round. The content and knowledge lane produced real conversations too, at Ashley Digital, Archive, and Fabric Health.

And the close. World Wide Technology's recruiter reached out after an earlier rejection for a different role. The process ran recruiter screen, hiring manager, a seven-person technical panel, a presentation on using skills files as part of an AI workflow, and an offer the same day, 2026-10-16. No interviewers are named here, by design. The companies showed up, and so did I.

How the Pipeline Actually Helped

Volume Without Burnout

Last time every application cost hours of manual tailoring and the math never worked. This time the pipeline made 414 tailored applications survivable over 19.3 weeks, and the score gate meant effort went where fit was real. The external picture explains why that matters: recruiters handle 93% more applications than in 2021 while hires per recruiter fell 43%, per Gem's 2026 benchmarks, and tailored resumes converted at 5.75% versus 2.68% for generic ones, per Huntr's Q2 2025 report. Tailoring was this pipeline's thesis, and someone else's data stands behind it.

The Artifacts Did Double Duty

The portfolio, the open-source repo, FlareGuard, CoSH, all built partly with the AI-augmented workflow, became interview proof rather than decoration. Recruiters mentioned the source code repo unprompted. The take-home and the final presentation were both answered with things I had actually built. The mechanism is measurable: sourced candidates reach interview at about 25% according to LinkedIn's applicant conversion data, several times the cold-inbound rate, and artifacts are what make a candidate sourceable. That is what happened when the WWT recruiter reached out. Not luck, a mechanism.

AI as a Sparring Partner, Not a Crutch

The market said no to the writer title and the workflow kept producing the next iteration: new resume angles, new portfolio pages, a repo that stayed audit-clean, this very tracker. Context: organizational AI adoption reached 88% per Stanford's 2026 AI Index and 52% of US workers now use AI in their role, per Gallup. Using AI was not the differentiator by 2026. Shipping with it was. The pipeline could not fix a closed lane. It made the open lane winnable and kept the search mechanical instead of emotional.

What It Did Not Do

It did not generate interviews from thin air. 209 applications never got a human reply, and part of that silence was structural: Ashby's own platform data found 18% of jobs were never filled. I will not pretend I know how many of my 209 were ghosts; nobody can. The honest claim is narrower and stronger. Same person, same skills, 12 months last time, 4.5 months this time, against more competition per posting and slower rehiring, because the system turned a search into a build. The pipeline made volume possible and the artifacts made conversations possible. Neither one forced an offer.

What the Market Taught Me

190 of 414 applications were rejected before anyone spoke to me. That ratio is the market's most honest statement, and one screen made it personal. One screen ended after five minutes. The company was Customer.io, the recorded role Technical Operations Manager. The recruiter asked what I had built, then asked whether I could code without AI. My honest answer, the one I would give again: everything I have coded was AI-assisted. I architect the direction while AI does the coding. The hiring manager's filter required coding without AI, a filter that was not in the job description, and the call ended. The role was rated strong on LLM, agentic systems, and prompt engineering in my own tracker.

One cousin of that story: the CivicPlus screen ran under a no-AI-tools-during-interview policy. Same era, same applicant, opposite instructions.

Then the counter-verdict that pairs with it. At World Wide Technology the technical interview was to use AI to solve a prompt while the team watched my workflow. Same builder, opposite verdicts, and the company that watched me work with AI hired me. That pairing is the responsible-AI payoff: guardrails and validation schemas are why AI-assisted is a strength, not a confession. Conversations came from demonstrated building, not from polish.

The story's closing irony: 93,151 of the 2026 tech layoffs were attributed to AI, and AI also built the portfolio, the repo, and the pipeline that got me hired. Keep it honest, not bitter.

Closing

I accepted the offer on 2026-10-16 and start 2026-11-02 as Senior Analyst, AI Native Engineering at World Wide Technology. The pipeline and this tracker are why the volume was survivable. The repo and the portfolio are why the conversations happened. The system is reusable, and it is already reused: the code is open, the method is written down, and the next search, if there is one, starts from a build instead of a blank page.
